# GEZHI — Make Understanding Visible

## Positioning

A 60-second English product launch film built around the Digital Space, the explanation process, six-dimensional evidence feedback, and explaining again. The final voiceover, interface, demo materials, subtitles, and end-card information are all in English.

Visual reference: Google's official Gemini 2.0 launch film (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fs0t6SdODd8). The shots, transitions, and audio design below are Gezhi's production plan, not conclusions from a frame-by-frame analysis of the reference film.

## Core innovations

1. Five roles use different follow-up questioning strategies: Peer, Teacher, Student, Examiner, Research Partner, so learners explain the same concept to different audiences.
2. Six-dimensional formative feedback includes quoted original words, the reason for the judgment, and a concrete next step; dimensions with no observable evidence show Not demonstrated and are not given partial credit. Quote-match validation is not the same as teacher scoring calibration.
3. Explanation, follow-up questioning, feedback, explaining again, and on-device history form a reusable process. Supporting demonstrations include confidence practice, Error Detective, and Concept Lab.

## English voiceover draft

Understanding begins when you explain why.

Meet Gezhi. A learning workspace built around teaching AI.

Choose a peer, teacher, student, examiner, or research partner. Each brings a different perspective to your explanation.

Speak or type. Use your own materials. Explore the questions that reveal missing conditions, unclear steps, and weak examples.

See your explanation across six dimensions: accuracy, conditions, reasoning, clarity, examples, and transfer. Every score links to your words and a concrete next step. Missing evidence stays unscored.

Keep your feedback. Try confidence checks, investigate errors, and test ideas in the Concept Lab. Then explain again.

Gezhi. Make understanding visible.

About 110–120 words, with pauses and stress refined at about 135 words per minute, leaving time for the music and for reading the visuals.

## Visuals and sound

- Deep graphite, soft white, pale purple, teal; large English type, fine lines, subtle material sheen, and clean depth of field.
- The UI stays real; depth comes from layering, parallax, and small camera moves. The main transitions are a card entering a room, the same quoted sentence entering the feedback, and a curve connecting to the experiment screen.
- Music is an original cinematic electronic score, or one licensed for competition use. 96 BPM, no lyrics, warm piano and delicate synths, restrained low frequencies; it peaks at the six-dimensional feedback and settles naturally at the end.
- Narration uses a natural, clear English voice; record a 10-second sample first to check pronunciation and pauses. The score ducks automatically under the narration, transition sound effects are light and short, and a loudness and peak check is performed at the end.

## Production and delivery

First complete the English demo interface, materials, and the actual AI feedback language, then record in a standalone window at a fixed resolution and fixed zoom level; use programmatic camera moves, motion graphics, and editing to composite.

Production check order: English sample and voice → animatic → full film → frame-by-frame check of text, transitions, and UI → sound mix → export.

Deliver a 60-second 4K / 60 fps MP4, plus a 1080p version and English SRT subtitles. All on-screen text is in English; titles and key sentences are integrated directly into the picture, avoiding stacking narration subtitles and UI at the same time.

Use real features and demo recordings of actual responses. Role evaluation has not been calibrated by teachers, and existing engineering validation does not replace real student trials or learning-effectiveness research.
